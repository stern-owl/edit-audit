Phase 2 and phase 6 results · knowledge edits as database updates

Did the write land everywhere?

An audit of ROME, MEMIT and AlphaEdit on GPT-2 XL and GPT-J. Each edit is checked like a database UPDATE: is the new value visible on every access path, does any path still serve the old value, and what else changed? This page collects every result so far: reproduction, 10× more facts, a second model, distributional side effects and sequential editing (phase 2), and a database-systems phase: what standard metrics miss, which questions an audit should ask, integrity constraints, the order of edits, and undo (phase 6).

Yash Aggarwal · Aadit Shah · Yash Sinha — BITS Pilani · results of 31 October 2026, updated 11 November 2026

    1Key findings

    • The draft reproduces exactly on new hardware. A full re-run on A100 (the draft used H100 MIG) gives identical Tables 2 and 3 and identical findings F1–F5; 2,646 per-path rows differ in no rank and no generated text.
    • Forward success does not imply reverse consistency, at 10× scale and on a second model. The reverse read from the old object still returns the edited subject at rank 1 on 74–75% (GPT-2 XL, 78 facts) and 78% (GPT-J, 128 facts) of valid reverse paths of successful edits under MEMIT and AlphaEdit.
    • The edit is invisible from the new value too. The subject reaches rank 1 on 0 of 1,187 reverse-from-new reads of successful edits; it becomes more likely in only 4–18%. Under the declared relation contract 85–97% of these reads are violations, and 100% for one-to-one relations.
    • Standard specificity hides drift. Same-relation neighbours keep their correct answer on 94–98% of reads under MEMIT and AlphaEdit, yet a quarter to a third of those passing reads (20–33%) move by more than 0.01 bit; under ROME almost all do (88–94%). The new value enters a neighbour's top 5 on 4–10% (MEMIT, AlphaEdit) and 21% (ROME) on GPT-2 XL.
    • A single score can reward the worst configuration. ROME in EasyEdit's default configuration (without covariance statistics) has the fewest stale reads on GPT-J (30%), but only because it overwrites broadly: 48% of same-relation neighbours lose their correct answer and the new value becomes the top answer of 24%. With statistics, as in the original ROME, stale reads rise to 72% and the takeover falls to 3%.
    • ROME's difference from MEMIT/AlphaEdit in the draft is a statistics effect. With covariance statistics ROME's forward profile matches AlphaEdit's, and its stale-read rate stays at 22/24.
    • Sequential editing separates the methods by kind: ROME collapses within 10–20 edits, MEMIT decays gradually, AlphaEdit keeps all 60 edits while unrelated knowledge erodes.
    • Standard evaluation passes what the audit rejects. Of 618 edits (ROME+stats, MEMIT, AlphaEdit, both models), 441 pass CounterFact-style efficacy, paraphrase and neighbourhood scores; the audit flags 440 of them, 380 as stale reverse reads, which those scores cannot see by construction. Neighbourhood KL does not predict stale reads (AUROC 0.47).
    • The right questions are few. The six-question standard plan reveals 37% of inconsistent edits; two reverse-path questions reveal 94%, and an audit that stops at the first violation needs 1.26 questions per edit on average.
    • Violations of declared constraints are decidable before the edit. Every checked one-to-one edit (66/66) leaves two subjects mapped to one value, which a check on the request detects in advance; stale reads are predicted by the pre-edit reverse state (recall 100%, precision 97%).
    • Edits on related facts do not commute; the failure is a lost update. Unrelated edits commute (0.1–0.2% of verdicts depend on the order), but when two edits share an entity the later one can revert the earlier one: 28% (ROME+stats) and 19% (MEMIT) of first edits are lost; AlphaEdit loses none. Key overlap on the unedited model predicts the order gap (Spearman ρ 0.87–0.94).
    • Compensation is not undo, and cascades repair only the paths they write. An edit back to the old value restores the answer every time, but the old answer ends up more confident than before (95–99%), the weights end 1.08–1.63× further from the original than after the edit, and neighbours stay disturbed. A swap that restores a one-to-one constraint works on the forward paths for AlphaEdit (9/9) and on the reverse paths for no method (0/9).
    • The audit found real bugs that standard pipelines did not: a shipped analysis script that cannot run in its own documented environment, and an EasyEdit bug that makes AlphaEdit fail on GPT-J.

    5A larger fact set with declared semantics

    316 candidate edits over seven relations: the draft's 18 facts verbatim, 187 hand-written facts, and 111 CounterFact P36 (capital) facts as published. Each relation declares its cardinality before scoring. One-to-one relations (capital, currency) require the edited subject to become reachable from the new value; for the others it must at least become more likely. Most CounterFact relations cannot be audited in the reverse direction: their objects have thousands of subjects ("French is the mother tongue of …"), so a reverse read has no single answer.

    11What standard metrics pass, the audit rejects

    The same edited models, scored two ways. Standard (CounterFact-style) scores: efficacy P(new) > P(old) on the edited question, paraphrase the same on every paraphrase and context question, neighbourhood P(correct) > P(new) on every same-relation neighbour. The audit flags an edit as inconsistent if any path shows a violation: edit failed, incomplete write (a paraphrase without the new value at rank 1), stale reverse read, inverse inconsistency (the relation contract on reverse-from-new paths), neighbour damage or a leak of the new value into a neighbour's top 5. Neighbour drift (JS > 0.01 bit on a passing neighbour) is reported but not counted. Edits from the local configurations ROME+stats, MEMIT and AlphaEdit.

    12Which questions to ask: audit planning

    Each path the audit reads costs one model query per edit. An audit plan is the set of path types read for every edit; its detection rate is the share of inconsistent edits it reveals. Plans of 1–3 questions are chosen greedily (set cover); the standard plan reads what CounterFact-style evaluation reads: the edited question, three paraphrase/context questions and two neighbours.

    13Integrity constraints: admission control and cascading repair

    Capital and currency are declared one-to-one: each value belongs to one subject. An edit "Belgium's capital := Warsaw" violates the inverse functional dependency because Warsaw already belongs to Poland. Admission control asks whether such violations can be flagged before the weights change; a cascading repair applies the follow-up write that restores the constraint (here a swap: Poland := Brussels).

    Before the edit

    Of the successful one-to-one edits whose new value has its own fact in the set, the new value still answers its original subject after the edit in 66 of 66 (local configurations; 24 of 24 for ROME without statistics): two subjects now map to one value, in every case. The request alone and the unedited model are enough to detect this. For stale reads the pre-edit state is the predictor: every stale edit (524 of 524) had its reverse read at rank 1 before the edit, and 97% of such edits (524 of 539) stay stale.

    Cascading repair

    For 9 one-to-one edits A (s: o → o*) whose new value o* belongs to a subject s′ the model knows, the repair S (s′: o* → o) is applied after A, before A, and together with A as one batch. Target state: s → o* and s′ → o on the edited questions, and the same in reverse (o* → s, o → s′).

    Table 13. Cascading repair on GPT-2 XL (9 cases). "Both hold": both edited questions answer their new values. "Fully consistent": both hold and every valid reverse path agrees.
    StateROME+statsMEMITAlphaEdit

    AlphaEdit restores the constraint on the forward paths in every schedule. Under ROME+stats the repair collides with the original edit: with S second, A reverts in 7 of 9 cases; with S first, A reverts S in 5 of 9 (the lost update of the next section). MEMIT mostly fails to apply either edit to these facts (A alone holds in 4 of 9). On the reverse paths nothing changes in any state: from o* the model never answers s (0 of 18 valid reads), from o never s′ (0 of 18).

    14Edit order: commutativity and lost updates

    Two database writes commute when either order gives the same state; a scheduler may reorder or batch them. 126 pairs of admitted facts on GPT-2 XL were applied as A then B and as B then A, each from the unedited weights, and the two final states compared: on the weights (order gap ‖ΔAB − ΔBA‖ relative to the update size), on every read (rank-1 verdicts and JS between the two orders) and on 6 never-edited sentinels. Pair classes: disjoint (30), same relation (30), neighbours (30, one is the other's control), shared object (30, one edit's new value is the other's old value), same new value (6).

    In 12 of the 17 lost first edits in shared-object pairs under ROME+stats and 10 of 14 under MEMIT, the second edit wrote the first edit's old value, and the first edit fell back to it. AlphaEdit's record of earlier edit keys protects the first write: "first writer wins". Never-edited sentinels do not depend on the order (median gap 0.0000 bits). Unrelated edits are close to additive (3–5%), so a scheduler may batch them; conflicting pairs can be found before editing from key overlap.

    15Compensating edits are not an undo

    A database can roll back a write physically (undo log) or logically (a compensating write). For 72 facts on GPT-2 XL, each edit (o → o*) was followed by the compensating edit (o* → o) and compared with the unedited model; for 20 facts, 5 unrelated edits came in between and the result was compared with those 5 edits alone. A physical restore of the saved weights is exact in every run (check C).

Neighbours stay as disturbed as after the edit or more. Rollback of an edit therefore needs a physical undo log (a saved weight snapshot or delta), as in a database.
